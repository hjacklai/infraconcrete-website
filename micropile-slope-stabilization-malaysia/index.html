01 / The in-situ reinforcement principle

How a micropile wall stabilizes a slope.

A micropile wall (or "in-situ reinforcement row", or by its Italian name "pali radice") is a row of small-diameter grouted piles installed through the sliding soil mass and anchored into stable ground beyond the failure surface. Each pile provides shear and bending resistance across the potential slip plane; the pile group collectively reinforces the slope.

Unlike a retaining wall, there is no excavation of the slope face. The piles are drilled from the slope surface (or from above) into the ground; grout and reinforcement are placed; the slope remains intact. This is decisive when the slope is active, when excavation would further destabilize it, when access from the toe is impossible, or when the asset at the slope crest must not be disturbed.

Design method: FHWA-NHI-05-039 Section 5 (Pali Radice slope stabilization) and FHWA-RD-98-065. Capacity governed by: (a) shear capacity of micropile group across slip surface, (b) bond strength in stable ground beyond slip surface, (c) overall slope stability factor of safety including micropile contribution.

02 / When micropile wall is the right choice

Six decisive situations.

  • Active slope movement where excavation would accelerate failure.
  • Marginal factor of safety (FoS 1.0-1.2) where additional restraint is needed without major earthwork.
  • Asset at slope crest (building, road, utility) that cannot be removed or disturbed.
  • Access from toe impossible (river, highway, building, boundary line at the bottom).
  • Soft or weak overburden over competent bedrock, where piles can transfer shear through the sliding mass into solid rock.
  • Environmental or aesthetic constraints that preclude a visible retaining wall or large excavation.
03 / The hybrid system

Micropile wall + soil nailing + drains.

On Malaysian hillside remediation, a micropile wall rarely stands alone. The typical hybrid system has three layers:

  1. Horizontal drains installed first to depressurise the slope. Reduces pore pressure within the sliding mass, improves effective stress, and often adds 10-20 percent to the slope factor of safety on its own.
  2. Micropile wall through the toe or mid-slope of the sliding mass, providing shear reinforcement across the active failure surface.
  3. Soil nailing above the micropile row, reinforcing the upper slope mass to prevent shallow translational failure and keep load away from the pile crest.

Our soil nailing capability base is directly transferable to the micropile wall above: same drilling methodology, same grouting equipment, same QA framework, same vibration monitoring. This is why micropile is a natural extension of our geotechnical scope rather than a new discipline.

04 / Design method

Six-step process.

  1. Characterise the slope: borehole investigation, inclinometer monitoring to identify active slip surface depth, water table, undrained shear strength of sliding mass.
  2. Baseline stability analysis: Bishop or Spencer slip-circle calculation of existing factor of safety.
  3. Micropile wall layout: row position (toe, mid-slope, or crest), pile spacing (typically 1.5-3 m), pile depth (through slip surface + 3-6 m bond length into stable ground), pile diameter (200-300 mm typical for slope walls).
  4. Pile capacity check: shear and bending capacity across slip surface per FHWA-NHI-05-039 and Pali Radice method.
  5. Combined stability analysis: rerun slip-circle with micropile group contribution added. Target FoS typically 1.3-1.5 for permanent works.
  6. Verification: inclinometer monitoring continues through and after construction to confirm slope movement arrested.
